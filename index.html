About

I'm a physicist with a background in electrical and electronics engineering working at the boundary of two-dimensional (2D) materials (that are only a few atoms thick) and photonics — nanofabrication and characterizing 2D materials based devices, then studying how they interact with light, followed by electrically probing this interaction. In my postdoctoral work I am leading two projects, one of which looks at 2D magnetic materials based tunnel junctions, which opens a new paradigm of magnon-based devices that couple light and magnetism on a single chip, and the other involves optimisation of a novel method of CVD graphene transfer for scalable, continuous, and reproducible adaptation in commercial photonic integrated circuits.

Before Sherbrooke, I completed my PhD at Trinity College Dublin, Ireland, developing pixelated stacks of transition metal dichalcogenides forming a Fabry–Pérot cavity for tuning the refractive index of visible light. That work sits at the intersection of materials deposition, nanofabrication, and optical measurement — the same combination I bring to my current research.

What draws me to this work? In the world of physics, light feels purely magical to me. Its mere existence gives life, nourishment, and technological resources for humans to tread upon. It is eternal — it has always been there and will always be — we just need to harvest its magic.

Research

Current focus areas
2D Magnets

Photocurrent effects in chromium trihalides

Investigating shift current, MOKE, and magnetic photogalvanic effects in 2D magnetic materials, presently working with CrCl₃ and evaluating which of these effects are worth pursuing given its comparatively weak spin–orbit coupling.

Device fabrication

CVD graphene transfer & heterostructure assembly

Growing and transferring CVD graphene and building 2D material heterostructures for low-temperature optical and electronic measurement.

Measurement

Cryogenic photocurrent & optical characterization

Measuring photoresponse of 2D material devices at cryogenic temperature to connect optical signatures back to underlying magnetic and electronic structure.
